\begin{abstract}
Thermal comfort in buildings has long been described through physiological and heat-balance models, yet how people actually seek, avoid, and use cooling is more variable than those models predict, especially in warm climates where air conditioning is pervasive.
This paper asks whether individuals can be grouped by their thermal-preference behavior rather than by physiology alone, using a longitudinal smartwatch micro-survey deployment collected in Singapore with the open-source Cozie platform.
The analysis draws on 9{,}520 geolocated thermal-preference responses from 104 eligible participants, each paired with a matched outdoor heat index and an environmental-control context defined by the likelihood that the occupied space is air-conditioned.
Three person-level behaviors, the tendency to prefer cooler conditions, the tendency to prefer warmer conditions, and the sensitivity of cooling demand to outdoor heat, are stabilized with empirical-Bayes shrinkage and clustered into four behavioral archetypes: the Craver, the Captive, the Conventional, and the Carefree.
Mechanistic scrutiny with cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals shows that two archetypes behave as thermal physiology predicts, with cooling demand that rises with outdoor heat and is relieved by air conditioning, while two are largely decoupled from the physical environment, consistently seeking or forgoing cooling regardless of the heat around them.
The groups are robust to clustering perturbations and are not explained by wearable physiology or by most stable personal traits, with only personality openness showing a modest association.
These findings indicate that thermal preference in a warm climate is only partly a physiological response and is substantially behavioral, and they argue for comfort strategies and cooling policies that offer people a genuine choice among low-carbon paths to feeling comfortable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================================

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering

  \caption{Elements shaping individual thermal preference. Physical, environmental, and physiological influences are shown on the left, with psychological, cultural, economic, and historical or institutional influences on the right.}
  \label{fig:thermal-preference-elements}
\end{figure*}

Characterization of thermal comfort in the built environment has long been dominated by the creation of thermal comfort models, which combine physiology and environmental science to describe when and how a person should feel thermally comfortable or uncomfortable \citep{Fanger1972-xq}.
Rooted in heat-balance theory, these models relate metabolic heat production, clothing insulation, air temperature, radiant conditions, humidity, and air movement to a predicted comfort state, drawing on how core and skin temperatures jointly drive thermal sensation \citep{Frank1999-ju}, how circadian phase and the thermoneutral zone set the body's baseline \citep{Krauchi2002-aq, Kingma2012-lm}, and how radiant and solar exposure adds heat that simple air-temperature metrics miss \citep{Halawa2014-ek, Hodder2007-qo}.
From these inputs, the models predict thermal sensation, satisfaction, and preference, and they remain the backbone of the comfort standards and mechanical systems designed around them \citep{Cheung2019-fs}.
Newer adaptive formulations extend this picture by recognizing that people stay comfortable across a wider range of conditions than heat-balance theory alone would predict, as behavioral adjustment, expectation, and prior thermal history reshape what feels acceptable \citep{Dear1998-va, Schweiker2020-pw}.
Even within the same space, individuals differ systematically in thermal sensitivity and response \citep{Rupp2022-lq, Wang2018-aj}.
To capture this variation directly, personal comfort models learn from an individual's own repeated feedback and physiological signals rather than assuming a population average \citep{Arakawa_Martins2022-vh}.
Yet both approaches face practical limits.
Generalized models predict individual thermal sensation correctly only about a third of the time \citep{Cheung2019-fs}, while personal models are difficult to scale because they depend on collecting sustained, labeled data from every occupant \citep{Arakawa_Martins2022-vh}.
Efforts to connect these models to the broader context that surrounds them, including the psychological and non-physical influences that shape perception \citep{Castaldo2018-ih, Chinazzo2019-ye}, demographic and cultural differences in expectation and preference \citep{Karjalainen2012-ag, Naheed2021-ce}, and the social conventions and economics that govern who controls and pays for indoor climate \citep{Shove2003-lf, Gillingham2012-nf}, remain nascent, but they may explain much of why the models still fall short.

Given these deficiencies, it is worth looking more closely at the wider set of factors that shape people's thermal experience in cities. 
Figure~\ref{fig:thermal-preference-elements} organizes them into two broad families.
The left side gathers the physical, environmental, and physiological factors that classical models were built to handle but that continue to surprise in their detail and interaction.
At the environmental scale, real spaces are rarely uniform, and local asymmetries in radiant, surface, and air-movement conditions shape comfort in ways a single room temperature cannot represent \citep{Su2023-tg}, while personally controlled air movement can restore comfort and even cognitive performance at warmer set points \citep{Schiavon2017-fx, Porras-Salazar2024-mo}.
Closer to the body, near-body heat sources such as laptops \citep{Zhang2014-im}, the insulation and cut of clothing \citep{Havenith2015-yn}, posture \citep{Tikuisis1996-fd}, and physical activity and metabolic rate \citep{Gaesser1975-jn} all shift the balance between heat production and loss.
The physiological state of the occupant adds further variation through cutaneous blood flow \citep{Johnson2010-eh}, sweating and skin wettedness \citep{Fukazawa2009-nx}, hydration \citep{Sawka2001-sz}, the thermic effect of food and drink \citep{Swaminathan1985-ca, Otsuka2021-ty}, and age-related shifts in thermal thresholds \citep{Huang2010-hy}.
Even the pleasantness of a given thermal stimulus depends on internal state, so the same temperature can feel welcome or aversive depending on whether it helps restore the body's balance \citep{Cabanac1971-mg}.
These physical and physiological signals also rarely act alone, combining with the visual, acoustic, and air-quality exposures that any rigorous account of the indoor experience must weigh together \citep{Chinazzo2022-dt}.

The right side of Figure~\ref{fig:thermal-preference-elements} gathers the psychological, cultural, economic, and historical or institutional forces that sit outside the heat balance yet strongly condition how people interpret and act on it.
Psychologically, cooling choices are bound up with identity, values, and the situation at hand rather than temperature alone \citep{Osunmuyiwa2020-fr}, and habitual reliance on air conditioning can harden into a practice that people find difficult to step outside of, even briefly \citep{Hitchings2011-eo}.
Culturally, expectations of what a comfortable indoor climate should feel like are learned and socially organized, shaped by evolving conventions, domestic technology, and the material culture of cooling \citep{Shove2014-fx, Strengers2011-bm, Goodchild2020-ew}, and they take on distinctive forms in specific settings, from the air-conditioned lives of young people in Singapore \citep{Hitchings2008-ky} to the use and energy demand of naturally ventilated places of worship \citep{Atmaca2019-ur}.
Economically, the decision to buy and run cooling responds to weather, prices, incomes, and the efficiency of the available equipment \citep{He2022-rg, Noonan2015-uz, Yun2011-nu}, and as incomes rise and the climate warms, adoption is projected to grow steeply, with large and unequally distributed consequences for electricity demand and emissions \citep{Davis2015-po, Colelli2023-fy, Falchetta2024-ao, Zhang2026-cx}.
Underlying all of this is a historical and institutional legacy in which particular comfort norms, standards, and infrastructures were exported and standardized, homogenizing expectations across very different climates and cultures \citep{Roaf2026-kt, Williamson2025-nt, Healy2008-hk, Winter2013-tm}, a trajectory whose sustainability in an increasingly hot world is now openly in question \citep{Lundgren-Kownacki2018-bv}.

\subsection{Repeated in-situ data can reveal behavioral response patterns}\label{sec:longitudinal-data}

Capturing the convergence of these physical and non-physical influences is difficult in controlled or laboratory settings, where the very context that shapes perception is stripped away.
Observing people in their natural environments over time is one of the few ways to see how thermal experience unfolds across the varied spaces, activities, and social situations of daily life.
Repeated in-situ reporting through ecological momentary assessment provides a practical route to these observations, recording subjective states as they occur rather than in recollection \citep{Stone1994-he}.
The data analyzed here come from exactly such a deployment, collected in Singapore with the open-source Cozie smartwatch platform, which delivers micro-surveys directly on an Apple Watch and pairs each response with the wearer's onboard physiological signals and linked outdoor weather \citep{Tartarini2023-iu}.
The deployment grew out of a broader program that used smartwatch-based just-in-time adaptive interventions to nudge heat and noise mitigation in the urban environment \citep{Miller2022-dy, Miller2025-sv}, and the resulting dataset has since supported a public machine-learning competition on predicting heat and noise perception from digital-twin context \citep{Miller2023-wi, Miller2025-qu} as well as related work linking wearable reports to urban digital twins \citep{Ignatius2024-ii, Lei2026-dn}.
Previous research characterizes this same deployment of 9{,}962 geolocated micro-survey responses from 106 participants, showing that everyday urban spaces separate most sharply along behavioral and acoustic lines and that thermal preference tracks the immediate controlled environment more than the outdoor weather \citep{Miller2026-cs}.
That space-level characterization motivates the present study, which expands the behavioral dimension of the same dataset by shifting the unit of analysis from the space to the person.
Rather than asking how perception differs between spaces, this study clusters participants by their repeated thermal-preference behavior to uncover person-level archetypes, and then examines whether participant metadata offers clues to the non-physical and environmental elements of their thermal experience.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================


This method builds directly on a smartwatch micro-survey deployment that was developed and reported across several prior studies, summarized here before detailing the archetype analysis that is new to this work.
The data were collected in Singapore with the open-source Cozie platform, an Apple Watch application that administers short micro-surveys and links each response to the wearer's onboard physiological signals and to matched outdoor weather \citep{Tartarini2023-iu}.
The deployment itself grew out of a smartwatch-driven just-in-time adaptive intervention framework for building occupants \citep{Miller2022-dy}, and its instrumentation, recruitment, and preliminary results have been described in the methods and competition papers that accompanied it \citep{Miller2023-wi, Miller2025-qu}.
Participants were recruited in and around the National University of Singapore under approval from its Institutional Review Board, gave informed consent, and wore a paired Apple Watch and iPhone during working hours for approximately four weeks while completing repeated micro-surveys \citep{Miller2026-cs}.
The resulting privacy-protected dataset of 9{,}962 geolocated responses from 106 participants was released publicly for a machine-learning competition \citep{Miller2023-wi, Miller2025-qu} and is characterized at the level of urban space in the companion paper \citep{Miller2026-cs}.
Within this dataset, the 9{,}520 responses that carry both a thermal-preference answer and a matched outdoor heat index form the basis for the present analysis, with a median of 96 responses per participant across the cohort.
Reported preferences skew toward no change, cooler preferences are far more common than warmer ones, most responses come from the home, and the matched outdoor heat index is high and narrow around a median near $34\,^{\circ}$C, consistent with the warm tropical setting and characterized more fully in the companion paper \citep{Miller2026-cs}.
Here the same underlying deployment is reused but analyzed at the level of the person, and the subsections below describe the survey measures and environmental-control context, the participant eligibility screen and person-level features, the archetype-discovery procedure, and the robustness checks used to scrutinize the resulting groupings.

\subsection{Survey measures, outdoor heat, and environmental-control context}\label{sec:measures}
At each submission, the Cozie micro-survey presents a short battery of questions that records the participant's thermal preference and the level of nearby noise, together with conditional follow-ups on the kind of noise and earphone use, the location or space type, whether the participant is \emph{Alone} or in a \emph{Group}, and the activity being performed \citep{Miller2026-cs}.
Figure~\ref{fig:cozie-interface} shows the two survey questions that this analysis uses as its primary components, thermal preference and location.
The remainder of the battery, along with the linked physiological, weather, and urban-context data, is treated as outside the scope of this study.
The thermal-preference question asks whether a participant would prefer to be \emph{Cooler}, feel \emph{No change}, or prefer to be \emph{Warmer}, and it is answered on the watch alongside the companion question that records the category of space they occupy at that moment.
Thermal preference is the primary behavioral outcome throughout this study because it captures the desired direction of change rather than a sensation label, which maps more directly onto the adaptive actions a person might take.
The location question resolves each response to an everyday space type, such as home, office, classroom, transport, and outdoor settings, so that preferences can be read in the context where they were expressed.
Figure~\ref{fig:preference-mix-and-by-space} summarizes the resulting survey composition, showing the overall balance of the three preference responses, how many surveys come from each space type, and how the preference mix shifts from one setting to another.
To situate each response in its thermal context, each is joined to a one-hour mean outdoor heat index derived from matched local weather, which serves as an exposure proxy for the ambient conditions around the participant rather than a direct measurement of their individual microclimate.
Because indoor spaces differ in how tightly their climate is controlled, each response is also assigned to an a-priori environmental-control context based on the likelihood that the occupied space is air-conditioned: outdoor settings are treated as having no air conditioning, home as possibly air-conditioned, offices, classrooms, and other indoor spaces as likely air-conditioned, and transport as very likely air-conditioned.
This grouping makes it possible to ask whether a preference was expressed in a setting where the occupant could plausibly act on it through mechanical cooling, and it provides the control-context axis used in the mechanistic analysis that follows.


\subsection{Participant eligibility and person-level features}\label{sec:features}
Clustering participants requires a stable behavioral signal from each person, so the survey-level dataset is first restricted to participants who contributed enough responses on both sides of the heat range.
Every response is labeled hot-side or cool-side relative to the cohort median of the one-hour outdoor heat index, which falls at $34.1\,^{\circ}$C, so that each participant can be summarized by how their preferences differ between warmer and cooler exposures.
A participant qualifies for the analysis if they submitted at least 15 responses in total, with at least 5 on the hot side and at least 5 on the cool side, applied across all locations rather than the home alone.
This all-location screen retains 104 of the 106 participants, or 98\%, dropping only the two who lacked sufficient coverage on one side of the split.

For each qualifying participant, three person-level features are constructed that summarize their thermal-preference behavior.
The first two are the rates at which they prefer to be cooler and prefer to be warmer, computed across all of their responses and stabilized with empirical-Bayes Beta--Binomial shrinkage, which fits a cohort-wide Beta prior by marginal maximum likelihood and pulls each participant's rate toward that prior in proportion to how few responses they contributed.
This shrinkage prevents participants with sparse records from producing extreme rates that would otherwise dominate the clustering.
Figure~\ref{fig:eb-shrinkage} shows the fitted cohort priors and how the shrinkage pulls each participant's raw rates toward those priors.
The third feature is a within-participant heat sensitivity, defined as the difference between their prefer-cooler rate on the hot side and on the cool side, $P(\mathrm{Cooler}\mid\mathrm{hot}) - P(\mathrm{Cooler}\mid\mathrm{cool})$, and left unshrunk so that it reflects each person's raw responsiveness to outdoor heat.
Together these features place each participant in a compact behavioral space of how much they seek cooling, how much they seek warmth, and how strongly their cooling demand tracks outdoor heat, which the next subsection uses to discover the archetypes.

\subsection{Archetype discovery, interpretation and scrutiny analysis}\label{sec:clustering}
With each qualifying participant reduced to the three behavioral features, the features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance so that no single rate dominates the distance metric, and participants are grouped with k-means clustering.
To choose the number of archetypes without over-fitting, candidate solutions from two to six clusters are swept and evaluated by their mean silhouette width, using many restarts and a fixed random seed for determinism.
The number of archetypes to carry forward is selected by balancing silhouette cohesion against interpretability, rather than treating the silhouette optimum as definitive.
The resulting groups are then interpreted through their standardized feature centroids and a principal-component projection of the feature space, alongside per-cluster silhouette widths that indicate how internally coherent each group is.
Because the underlying features vary continuously, the groups are treated as descriptive behavioral summaries rather than categorical types, and membership is expected to be soft near the boundaries between them.
The number of archetypes carried forward, their standardized signatures, and their descriptive names are presented in the Results.



Beyond discovering the archetypes, they are subjected to a mechanistic scrutiny that tests whether each group behaves as its signature implies.
The dose-response of thermal preference to outdoor heat is examined by binning responses across the observed heat range, and preferences are compared across the a-priori environmental-control contexts, focusing on the gradient between likely air-conditioned and outdoor settings.
These contrasts are quantified with distribution-free effect sizes, principally Cliff's delta, and uncertainty is attached with participant-level bootstrap confidence intervals rather than relying on p-values.
The stability of the solution is probed by re-clustering across random seeds and subsamples and by confirming that membership agrees under alternative outdoor-heat thresholds.
Because survey observations are nested within participants and were not experimentally assigned, every comparison is treated as descriptive and associational rather than causal.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering

  \label{fig:archetype-overview}

Applying the person-level feature construction and clustering to the 104 eligible participants yields four behavioral thermal-preference archetypes, summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:archetype-overview}.
The four groups, named the Craver, the Captive, the Conventional, and the Carefree, differ in how often they seek cooling, how often they report being too cold, and how strongly their preferences track outdoor heat.
Figure~\ref{fig:archetype-overview} summarizes how the four archetypes differ along the three behavioral features, and Figure~\ref{fig:centroid-profiles} quantifies these differences as standardized signatures.
The Craver, or Constant Cool-Seekers (n = 23, 22.1\%), prefers cooler conditions in roughly 74\% of responses and carries a below-average heat sensitivity, seeking cooling whether or not outdoor heat is high.
The Captive, or Often Over-Cooled (n = 12, 11.5\%), stands apart through a much higher prefer-warmer rate, near 21\% against only a few percent in the other groups, indicating that this group reports being too cold far more often than any other.
The Conventional, or Normatively Responsive (n = 35, 33.7\%), the largest group, shows the strongest heat sensitivity, so its prefer-cooler response rises and falls with outdoor heat in the textbook thermoregulatory pattern.
The Carefree, or Heat-Unbothered (n = 34, 32.7\%), sits below the cohort mean on all three features and reports no change in roughly 77\% of responses, remaining comfortable even outdoors at comparable heat exposure.

\subsection{Heat dose-response and environmental-control context}\label{sec:mechanistic-results}
The four archetypes were derived from behavior alone, so a mechanistic scrutiny examines whether each group behaves as its signature implies once responses are placed back in their thermal and environmental context.
The scrutiny proceeds through a coherent sequence: how far each group's cooling demand is relieved as spaces become more likely to be air-conditioned, the anatomy of over-cooling in the most conditioned settings, the dose-response of preference to outdoor heat, and the balance between heat tolerance and heat exposure.
Throughout, uncertainty is reported as 95\% cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals that resample whole participants, and group contrasts are summarized with distribution-free effect sizes, so that no comparison rests on participant-nested responses being treated as independent.
Because participants were not assigned to contexts or heat levels, every result is read as descriptive and associational rather than causal.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/01_A3_relief_by_context_5cat.pdf}
  \caption{Prefer-cooler responses by archetype across probable control contexts ordered by increasing air-conditioning likelihood, with 95 percent cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals.}
  \label{fig:ac-relief}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:ac-relief} shows the share of prefer-cooler responses for each archetype across five settings ordered by their air-conditioning likelihood, from outdoors with no air conditioning to offices, classrooms, and transport that are likely air-conditioned.
For three of the four groups the prefer-cooler share falls as spaces become more likely to be air-conditioned, the signature of relief from mechanical cooling: the Conventional drops from about 62\% outdoors to 33\% in offices and classrooms, the Captive from 53\% to 23\%, and the Carefree, already the most heat-tolerant, from 32\% to 11\%.
The Craver is the exception, holding a high prefer-cooler share of roughly 64 to 82\% across every context, including the spaces most likely to be air-conditioned, which points to a cooling demand that mechanical conditioning does not satisfy.
The confidence intervals are wider for the smaller groups, particularly the Captive, but the separation of the Craver above the other archetypes in the likely air-conditioned settings is consistent.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/01_B1_overcooling_anatomy.pdf}
  \caption{Prefer-warmer responses by archetype across probable control contexts ordered by increasing air-conditioning likelihood.}
  \label{fig:overcooling-anatomy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:overcooling-anatomy} isolates the too-cold response by plotting the prefer-warmer share of each archetype across the control contexts.
The Captive is the only group that reports being too cold with any regularity, preferring warmer conditions in about 20 to 28\% of responses depending on the setting, against roughly 1 to 7\% for every other archetype.
Its over-cooling is most pronounced in the likely air-conditioned offices, classrooms, and other indoor spaces, where the prefer-warmer share reaches 28\%.
The within-Captive gradient from outdoors to these likely air-conditioned settings is positive, at about 7 percentage points, but its 95\% cluster-bootstrap interval is wide and includes zero, so the anatomy of over-cooling is clear in level while its context gradient stays uncertain in this small group of twelve participants.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/01_C3_dose_response_heat_3super.pdf}
  \caption{Probability of preferring cooler conditions across outdoor heat-index sextiles and archetypes, stratified by probable control context from lowest (top) to highest (bottom) air-conditioning likelihood, with Wilson 95 percent intervals and the cohort median heat index.}
  \label{fig:heat-dose-response}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:heat-dose-response} turns to the dose-response of cooling demand, tracing the prefer-cooler share against outdoor heat across sextiles within three control supergroups.
Only the Conventional shows a clear upward slope, with its prefer-cooler share rising by about 18 percentage points between its coolest and hottest responses (95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 11 to 25 points), the signature of a group whose comfort tracks the weather.
The Craver stays high and nearly flat, the Carefree stays low and nearly flat, and the Captive is flat as well, with heat couplings that are statistically indistinguishable from zero.
The upward slope is clearest in the outdoor supergroup, where outdoor heat varies most and no mechanical cooling intervenes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/01_C2_heat_coupling.pdf}
  \caption{Difference in participants' prefer-cooler probability between hot and not-hot observations by archetype, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals.}
  \label{fig:heat-coupling}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:heat-coupling} distills this dose-response into a single heat-coupling value per archetype, the difference in prefer-cooler probability between hot and cooler responses.
Only the Conventional has a coupling distinguishable from zero, at about $+0.18$ (95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 0.11 to 0.25), while the Craver, Captive, and Carefree sit near zero with intervals that span it, confirming that only one archetype's cooling demand moves with outdoor heat.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/01_D1_exposure_provoked_comfort.pdf}
  \caption{Outdoor-exposure share (left) and probability of reporting No Change during hot outdoor observations (right) by archetype.}
  \label{fig:exposure-provoked-comfort}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:exposure-provoked-comfort} asks whether the Heat-Unbothered Carefree simply avoid heat or genuinely tolerate it.
The left panel shows that outdoor-exposure share is statistically indistinguishable across the four archetypes, near 10 to 11\% for each (Kruskal-Wallis $p = 0.88$), so no group is systematically sheltered from the heat.
The right panel shows that even when responses are hot or outdoors, the Carefree report no change far more often, in about 76\% of hot responses and 62\% of outdoor responses, against 24\% and 21\% for the Craver, 41\% and 26\% for the Captive, and 45\% and 38\% for the Conventional.
Read together with their equal exposure, this points to genuine heat tolerance in the Carefree rather than avoidance of hot conditions.


\subsection{Robustness and boundary membership}\label{sec:robustness-results}


\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/01_E1_independent_behaviours.pdf}
  \caption{Survey characteristics not used to construct the archetypes: space type, noise distraction, and social context (left to right).}
  \label{fig:independent-behaviors}
\end{figure*}

Several checks probe whether the four archetypes are a robust behavioral construct rather than an artifact of the clustering.
The archetypes are strongly separated on the very features that define them, with pairwise Cliff's delta on the prefer-cooler rate approaching its extremes of $\pm1$ between the most distinct groups.
The archetypes also vary in survey characteristics that were never used in the clustering, namely the mix of space types, noise distraction, and alone-versus-group context, consistent with a genuine behavioral construct rather than a clustering artifact (Figure~\ref{fig:independent-behaviors}).

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/01_E3_hr_and_movement_by_archetype.pdf}
  \caption{Participant-level wearable physiology during the hour before each survey by archetype: resting heart-rate proxy, mean heart rate, heart-rate standard deviation, and movement (left to right). None differs significantly across archetypes (Kruskal-Wallis epsilon-squared $< 0.03$).}
  \label{fig:hr-movement}
\end{figure*}

By contrast, the archetypes are undifferentiated on wearable physiology in the hour before each survey, with Kruskal-Wallis effect sizes at or below 0.024 for the resting heart-rate proxy, mean heart rate, heart-rate variability, and movement, and none of the differences statistically significant (Figure~\ref{fig:hr-movement}).
This null is reassuring, because thermal preference is a perceptual and behavioral signal rather than a simple function of heart rate or activity.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/02_B1_openness_vs_null_dimensions.pdf}
  \caption{Participant-level onboarding measures by archetype (n = 101): openness, life satisfaction, age, and body mass index (left to right). Openness is higher among the Carefree archetype (Kruskal-Wallis epsilon-squared = 0.137, $p = 0.003$), while the other measures overlap across groups.}
  \label{fig:onboarding-openness}
\end{figure*}

Among stable onboarding traits, only Ten-Item Personality Inventory Openness separates the archetypes at a medium effect size (epsilon-squared 0.137, $p = 0.003$), running highest in the Carefree, while the other personality dimensions, life satisfaction, sensory sensitivity, age, body mass index, years in Singapore, acclimatization, and gender all overlap across groups (Figure~\ref{fig:onboarding-openness}).

The partition itself is stable to reasonable perturbations, reproducing across fifty random seeds with a median adjusted Rand index of 0.94 and under repeated ninety-percent subsampling with a median adjusted Rand index of 0.77.
It is more sensitive to the choice of heat threshold, with adjusted Rand indices between about 0.47 and 0.65 when the hot-cool split is moved well away from the cohort median, which is expected because that threshold enters the feature construction itself.
Together these results support reading the archetypes as a useful discretization of a continuous behavioral space rather than a deterministic classifier that would assign every new individual to a fixed type.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Role of behavioral archetypes in thermal comfort research for the built environment}\label{sec:contribution}
As outlined in the introduction, there is a long history of characterizing how the human body reacts to its thermal environment, from heat-balance models of comfort to the physiology of thermoregulation and cutaneous blood flow \citep{Fanger1972-xq, Johnson2010-eh, Frank1999-ju}.
This body of work is invaluable for describing, in theory, the conditions under which a person should feel comfortable, yet real-world thermal behavior is consistently more complex than the models predict \citep{Cheung2019-fs, Dear1998-va}.
The link between what a person's body senses and what that person then decides to do, whether to seek, purchase, or operate cooling, is shaped by expectation, habit, and circumstance as much as by physiology \citep{Schweiker2020-pw, Osunmuyiwa2020-fr, Hitchings2011-eo}.
The behavioral archetypes recovered here make that gap concrete in a tropical setting.
Two of the groups behave largely as physiological theory would predict, as the Conventional track outdoor heat in the textbook thermoregulatory pattern \citep{Johnson2010-eh}, and the Captive resemble them but are more sensitive to the cold of air-conditioned interiors, an over-cooling that echoes reports of discomfort in aggressively conditioned tropical buildings \citep{Schiavon2017-fx, Rupp2022-lq}.
The other two groups are harder to reconcile with the physical environment alone, as the Craver consistently want cooling and the Carefree consistently do not, in each case largely decoupled from outdoor heat and from the controlled settings around them.
Preferences of this kind are unlikely to be explained by heat balance and instead point to the psychological, cultural, and economic factors on the right side of Figure~\ref{fig:thermal-preference-elements}, from expectation and the pleasantness of a stimulus given internal state \citep{Schweiker2020-pw, Cabanac1971-mg} to the social conventions and material cultures that normalize particular indoor climates \citep{Shove2003-lf, Shove2014-fx, Healy2008-hk}.
Isolating which of these factors drive the Craver and the Carefree will require field-based, longitudinal studies that combine behavioral measurement with the qualitative and mixed methods better suited to motivation and meaning \citep{Hitchings2011-eo, Strengers2011-bm}.

\subsection{Implications for the design and operations of buildings}\label{sec:implications}
These findings speak directly to how buildings are designed and operated, because the conventional approach is largely one-size-fits-all, provisioning a single set point that theory says should satisfy an average occupant \citep{Cheung2019-fs}.
A more holistic approach would combine several routes to comfort so that occupants can choose what works for them, whether through adaptive operation, personally controlled air movement, or personal comfort systems \citep{Dear1998-va, Schiavon2017-fx, Arakawa_Martins2022-vh}.
The residential context is where this matters most, since households themselves decide whether to install cooling and how to run it, decisions that respond to income, price, and habit rather than to physiology alone \citep{Davis2015-po, He2022-rg, Yun2011-nu}.
Air conditioning is a genuinely valuable technology in a warming climate, supporting sleep, health, and daytime productivity \citep{Porras-Salazar2024-mo, Miller2025-ne}, and the existence of the Conventional and Captive groups shows that much cooling demand is a real response to heat.
The concern is the Craver-like pattern of habitual, heat-independent cooling, which, scaled across a rapidly conditioning tropical belt, carries large consequences for energy use and carbon emissions \citep{Davis2015-po, Colelli2023-fy, Falchetta2024-ao, Zhang2026-cx}.
Addressing it will require partnering with social scientists and behavioral economists to understand cooling as a practice, and to find a balance between the habitual overuse of air conditioning and its habitual avoidance \citep{Shove2003-lf, Strengers2011-bm, Gillingham2012-nf}.
Greater thoughtfulness about when and where cooling is installed, together with a broader palette of technologies such as radiant systems, elevated air movement, adaptive clothing, and personal cooling, could deliver comfort to more of these archetypes at a fraction of the energy cost \citep{Halawa2014-ek, Schiavon2017-fx, Havenith2015-yn}.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
This work has several limitations that bound how far its findings should be read.
The environmental-control contexts are defined a priori from the likelihood that a space is air-conditioned, so statements about likely air-conditioned settings describe association with space type and must not be read as causal claims about air-conditioning operation, which was not measured directly.
The cohort is drawn from a single tropical city and is young and largely self-selected, which limits generalization to other climates, age groups, and cultures \citep{Naheed2021-ce, Karjalainen2012-ag}.
Because survey observations are nested within participants and were not experimentally assigned, every comparison is descriptive and associational rather than causal.
Outdoor weather serves as a proxy for each participant's thermal exposure rather than a direct measurement of their immediate microclimate, so indoor conditions and radiant effects are only partially captured \citep{Halawa2014-ek}.
The location and control-context assignments rest on self-reported space type and on the assumption that Singapore offices, classrooms, and transport are reliably air-conditioned, which holds in aggregate but not for every response.
The response distribution is imbalanced, with prefer-warmer votes especially rare, so the smaller archetypes such as the Captive are estimated with wider uncertainty.
Finally, the archetype names are descriptive labels applied after clustering, and membership is soft near the boundaries, so the four groups should be read as an interpretable discretization of a continuous behavioral space rather than as fixed types.

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:future-work}
Several directions would strengthen and extend this line of work.
The most important is replication in other climates and cohorts, with independent validation of whether a similar set of archetypes emerges beyond a young tropical sample \citep{Naheed2021-ce}.
Pairing the wearable micro-surveys with direct indoor environmental measurements, including air and radiant temperature and personal exposure, would remove the reliance on outdoor weather as a proxy and sharpen the environmental-control contrasts \citep{Halawa2014-ek}.
Following participants over longer periods would show whether archetype membership is stable or whether preferences drift as expectations and thermal history change \citep{Schweiker2020-pw, Dear1998-va}.
Moving beyond association will require designs that can evaluate causal control interventions, for example just-in-time adaptive interventions that nudge cooling behavior and measure the response \citep{Miller2025-sv}.
Explaining why the Craver and the Carefree diverge will ultimately depend on integrating the psychological, cultural, and economic disciplines represented on the right side of Figure~\ref{fig:thermal-preference-elements}, through the kind of mixed-methods collaboration that behavioral and social science bring \citep{Shove2003-lf, Strengers2011-bm, Gillingham2012-nf}.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
% =============================================================================
This study used a longitudinal smartwatch micro-survey deployment in Singapore, comprising 9{,}520 geolocated thermal-preference responses from 104 eligible participants, to derive a behavioral typology of thermal preference.
Clustering three person-level behaviors, the tendency to prefer cooler conditions, the tendency to prefer warmer conditions, and the sensitivity of cooling demand to outdoor heat, yielded four archetypes: the Craver, the Captive, the Conventional, and the Carefree.
Mechanistic scrutiny showed that two of these archetypes behave as thermal physiology would predict, with cooling demand that tracks outdoor heat and is relieved by air conditioning, while the other two are largely decoupled from the physical environment, consistently seeking or forgoing cooling regardless of the heat around them.
These patterns are robust to reasonable perturbations of the clustering and are not explained by wearable physiology or by most stable personal traits, with only personality openness showing a modest association.
Taken together, they show that thermal preference in a warm climate is only partly a physiological response and is substantially a matter of behavior, expectation, and habit, which argues for comfort strategies and cooling policies that give people a genuine choice among low-carbon paths to feeling comfortable.
